The Neon Prophet
by IIDZII

### Chapter 1: The Sermon on Seventh and Drench

The rain in Meridian City never really stopped. It paused sometimes, the way a predator pauses before the kill — a brief, mocking stillness that let you believe, for a few gasping minutes, that the sky might actually clear. Then it came again, heavier, as if the clouds had been reloading. Mira Calloway had lived in Meridian all thirty-one years of her life, and she'd stopped trusting the pauses around the same time she stopped trusting press releases.

She stood under the awning of a noodle shop on Seventh and Drench — an intersection so notoriously flooded that the city had given up fixing the drains and instead installed elevated walkways, which had also broken. Across the street, half-drowned in neon reflections from a pharmacy sign that flickered between "OPEN" and "PEN" in a way that felt prophetic, a man was preaching.

He wasn't like the other street preachers. Meridian had dozens of them — angry men with megaphones and sandwich boards, screaming about sin and judgement and the end of days, competing with the advertising drones for volume and credibility. This man had no megaphone. No sandwich board. No Bible, or Quran, or sutra, or any of the scriptures Mira had seen weaponized on street corners. He stood on an overturned crate in the rain, wearing a long coat the color of storm clouds, and he spoke in a voice that was barely above a conversation.

Yet she could hear him. Over the hiss of rain, over the grind of traffic, over the bass throb of a club three blocks away. His voice carried like something physical — a hand on the shoulder, a finger to the chest.

"Kensington-Vance will fall," he said. Not shouted. Said. "Not today. Not tomorrow. But in eleven days, their quarterly drops, and when it does, it will take three pension funds with it. Seventeen thousand people will lose their retirement. And no one in this city will blink, because blinking requires seeing, and Meridian has gone blind."

Mira's fingers tightened around her press badge. Kensington-Vance. The pharmaceutical conglomerate. She'd been trying to get a story on their off-books genetic trials for six months. Every lead evaporated. Every source clammed up. Every editor told her it was "speculative" and "unverifiable" and "not the kind of content our sponsors — " and then the word "sponsors" would hang in the air like a verdict.

She looked at the preacher more carefully. He was older than she'd first thought — maybe sixty, maybe seventy, with a face like eroded stone, deep lines carved by weather or worry or both. His eyes were wrong, though. They were young. Fierce. The kind of eyes that had seen something they couldn't unsee and had decided, against all reason, to keep looking.

"Who are you?" she muttered to herself.

As if he'd heard her — and maybe he had, the way he heard everything — the preacher turned his head. His gaze found hers across the rain-slicked street, across the parade of umbrellas and the river of reflected neon, and he smiled. Not a friendly smile. Not an unfriendly one. A smile that said: you're already too involved to walk away.

She hated that he was right.

### Chapter 2: The Pattern

Mira didn't sleep that night. She sat in her apartment on the 43rd floor of the Whitfield Residences — a building that had been luxury housing before the cracks started showing, and was now "character housing," which was real estate speak for "falling apart but the rent's still criminal" — and she researched.

The preacher's name, she discovered through three hours of trawling public records and a favor from a contact at the Municipal Data Office, was Gabriel Okonkwo. Born in Lagos. Emigrated to Meridian at nineteen. PhD in computational economics from Meridian Metropolitan University — a school that had lost its accreditation eight years ago in the Education Collapse, which meant the degree was technically worthless but the knowledge wasn't. No known employment for the last twelve years. No registered address. No digital footprint beyond a few archived university papers on predictive modeling and emergent behavior in financial systems.

That last part made her sit up.

She found the papers. Read them. Then read them again. Okonkwo's research had been about something he called "cascading disclosure" — the idea that large-scale corporate deception leaves mathematical signatures in market data, the way a submerged object leaves ripples on the surface of water. You couldn't see the object, but you could read the ripples. And if you could read them well enough, you could predict not just what was hidden, but when it would surface.

His thesis had been rejected. His advisors had called it "speculative" and "unverifiable" — the same words her editors used. He'd been asked to revise. He'd refused. He'd left academia and disappeared into the streets of Meridian, and now, twelve years later, he was standing on a crate in the rain, making predictions.

She started checking. His public sermons — there were recordings, fragments captured by passersby and uploaded to the city's chaotic social feeds — went back three years. And the predictions:

Omar Financials. Collapsed fourteen months ago. He'd predicted it three weeks before.

Brightline Logistics. Exposed for wage fraud eleven months ago. He'd called it a month before.

Hesperus Energy. The explosion at the Eastbank plant that killed forty workers. He'd said, two weeks prior: "Hesperus burns from the inside. The fire is already lit."

Every single one. Not vague prophecies that could be retrofitted to events — specific, dated, named predictions. The kind that shouldn't be possible without insider knowledge, except that insider knowledge required contacts, and Gabriel Okonkwo had no contacts. He had a crate and a coat and a voice that cut through rain.

Mira stared at her screen. The clock read 4:17 AM. Outside, the rain had resumed its eternal assault on the city. She had a choice: dismiss this as coincidence, or walk deeper into the flood.

She pulled on her coat and went to find him.

### Chapter 3: The Interview

She found him in the same spot. Seventh and Drench. As if he'd been waiting. The rain was lighter now — one of those predatory pauses — and the street was empty except for the preacher and a stray dog that sat at his feet like a congregant.

"Ms. Calloway," he said, before she'd introduced herself. "From the Meridian Standard. Although I suspect you're not here on assignment."

"I'm here because you predicted Kensington-Vance would drop in eleven days. That was four days ago. Seven days left, Mr. Okonkwo. If you're right, seventeen thousand people are about to lose everything."

"If I'm right, seventeen thousand people were going to lose everything regardless of whether I said it or not. The only question is whether they get warning."

"That's not how journalism works. We need verification. Sources. Evidence."

He looked at her with those too-young eyes. "You've had evidence for six months, Ms. Calloway. Your contacts at the medical board gave you the trial data. Your source inside Kensington-Vance confirmed the off-books operations. You have everything you need. What you don't have is permission."

The words hit her like cold water. "How do you — "

"How do I know what you know? The same way I know about Kensington-Vance. The ripples, Ms. Calloway. Every secret creates patterns. Every lie leaves a mathematical residue. I spent my career learning to read those residues, and when the university told me my work was too dangerous to publish — because the corporations that fund the university have the most secrets and the most lies — I took my work to the only place left where no one can silence it."

He gestured at the rain, at the street, at the indifferent city.

"The street corner."

"But you can't just — you can't stand on a crate and announce corporate collapses. People will think you're crazy. The companies will come after you. The city will — "

"The city will ignore me. That's the beauty of it. I'm a madman on a street corner. No one believes madmen. Except the madmen are the only ones telling the truth." He paused. The stray dog rested its chin on his boot. "You want evidence, Ms. Calloway? Here's your evidence: in seven days, at 9:43 AM, Kensington-Vance will release a statement admitting to unauthorized genetic trials on homeless subjects. The stock will open at twelve percent down and close at thirty-four percent down. The pension funds tied to their bond offering — the Municipal Workers Fund, the Teachers' Reserve, and the Harbor Retirement Trust — will be insolvent by end of week. Write it down. Publish it. And when it happens, ask yourself: why did the entire apparatus of journalism, regulation, and oversight fail to see what a man on a crate could see?"

"How can you be this specific? 9:43 AM? Thirty-four percent?"

"Because the ripples are precise, Ms. Calloway. The mathematics of deception are as exact as the mathematics of ballistics. A lie follows a trajectory. And I can calculate where it lands."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to poke holes in his certainty, to find the flaw in his logic that would let her dismiss him and go back to her comfortable skepticism. But she couldn't find one, because his certainty wasn't the certainty of faith — it was the certainty of mathematics, and mathematics didn't care whether you believed in it or not.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"Nothing. Everything. I want you to do what you were trained to do — report the truth. The difference is that I'm giving you the truth before it happens, and that makes it harder. Truth after the fact is journalism. Truth before the fact is prophecy. And nobody trusts prophets." He looked down at the dog, then back at her. "But you came here at four in the morning, in the rain, to talk to a man on a crate. That means you already trust me more than you trust your editors."

He was right again. She was starting to hate how often he was right.

Mira pulled out her phone and opened her notes app. She typed: "Okonkwo. Kensington-Vance. 9:43 AM. Seven days." Then she looked up at him.

"If you're wrong, I look like a fool."

"If I'm wrong, you go back to writing about zoning permits and celebrity gossip. No harm done. But if I'm right — " He let the sentence hang in the rain between them, unfinished and heavy with possibility.

"If you're right, everything changes."

"No, Ms. Calloway. If I'm right, everything has already changed. The question is whether you'll be the one who reports it, or the one who reads about it in someone else's byline."

She turned and walked away. But she didn't go home. She went to the office, sat at her desk, and began to write.

### Chapter 4: 9:43 AM

She didn't publish. She wanted to. She drafted the story three times, her fingers flying over the keyboard with the reckless joy of a journalist who's finally found the thread that unravels everything. But each time, she stopped at the same wall: her editor would kill it. Not because it was wrong, but because it was unverifiable by their standards. A street preacher's predictions weren't a source. They were a curiosity. A human interest piece, at best. "Crazy Man Predicts Doom — Film at Eleven."

So she waited. She watched. And on the seventh day, at 9:43 AM, Kensington-Vance released a statement.

The words were different from Okonkwo's prediction, but the substance was identical. Unauthorized trials. Homeless subjects. The stock opened at twelve percent down.

Mira was standing in the newsroom when the numbers came in. Around her, journalists scrambled — this was breaking news, the biggest story of the quarter, and everyone was caught flat-footed. Everyone except her. She'd known for seven days. She'd had the story for seven days. And she'd sat on it because the system told her it wasn't a story until the system said it was.

The stock closed at thirty-four percent down. The number flashed on every screen in the newsroom, and the sound that followed was the sound of thirty journalists realizing they'd been asleep at the wheel — a collective intake of breath, the kind that precedes either a scream or a prayer.

The Municipal Workers Fund announced insolvency that afternoon. The Teachers' Reserve and the Harbor Retirement Trust followed within forty-eight hours. Seventeen thousand people. Gone. Just like the man on the crate said.

Mira watched the fallout on every channel. Pensioners standing in lines that stretched around blocks, clutching statements that said their life savings had been reclassified as "unrecoverable assets." City council members giving press conferences with faces that were carefully arranged into expressions of shock, though their eyes — Mira had learned to read eyes, the way Okonkwo read ripples — told a different story. They'd known. Maybe not the specifics. Maybe not the timeline. But they'd known the shape of the disaster, and they'd chosen not to see it, because seeing it would have meant acting on it, and acting on it would have meant confronting the corporations that funded their campaigns.

She found her draft — the one she'd written and not published — still sitting in her drafts folder. She read it again. Every word was true. Every prediction was accurate. And every editor who would have killed it was now scrambling to assign reporters to a story that a woman in their own newsroom had already written, seven days before it broke.

She didn't feel vindicated. She felt sick.

She found him again. Same corner. Same rain. But this time, there was a crowd. Not a big one — maybe thirty people, huddled under umbrellas, watching him with the hungry, desperate eyes of people who'd just lost everything and were looking for someone to tell them it had been visible all along.

"They knew," Okonkwo was saying. "Not the details. Not the dates. But they knew the shape. They could have seen it, the way you can see a storm coming by reading the clouds. But reading clouds isn't profitable. Ignoring storms is. And so they chose not to see, because seeing would require action, and action would require change, and change is the one thing this city fears more than the rain."

Mira pushed to the front of the crowd. He saw her. That smile again — the one that said you're already too involved.

"Are you going to write about me now, Ms. Calloway?"

"I'm going to write about what you see. I'm going to write about the ripples. And this time, I'm not going to wait for permission."

Okonkwo regarded her for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat — a gesture that, in Meridian, could mean anything from a pamphlet to a weapon — and pulled out a small, water-stained notebook. He held it out to her.

"My calculations. Every ripple I've read for the last three years. Names, dates, trajectories. It's not evidence in the way your editors understand evidence. But it's proof that the patterns exist, and that they can be read by anyone willing to look."

She took the notebook. It was heavier than it looked, dense with pages of meticulous handwriting — numbers and symbols and arrows connecting data points like constellations. It was the most beautiful thing she'd ever held.

"Why give this to me?" she asked.

"Because I'm tired, Ms. Calloway. I've been standing on this crate for three years, and the rain hasn't stopped once. My voice carries, but it doesn't carry far enough. A newspaper — even a dying one — reaches more people than a man on a street corner ever will. You have amplifiers I don't. Use them."

She tucked the notebook inside her coat, against her chest, where it wouldn't get wet. "What's the next ripple?"

Okonkwo closed his eyes. When he opened them, the fierce young light in them had dimmed, just slightly, replaced by something that looked almost like grief.

"Meridian Energy Holdings. Six weeks. The biggest collapse this city has ever seen. And this time, it won't just be pensions. It'll be the grid itself. Three days without power in the middle of winter. People will die, Ms. Calloway. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. And the ripples have been visible for months."

Mira felt the weight of the notebook against her ribs. Six weeks. Enough time to write the story, if she could find the courage to publish it. Enough time to save lives, if she could find a way to make anyone listen.

The rain came down harder. The crowd pressed closer. And somewhere in the towers above them, in the glass offices where the real sermons were preached — the ones about growth and value and shareholder returns — the next lie was already forming its pattern, its ripples already spreading outward through the data, waiting for someone who could read them.

There were more prophets now. Not just Okonkwo. Others had started appearing on corners across the city — people who'd learned to see what the system was designed to make invisible. A retired accountant on Fifth and Monsoon who predicted supply chain failures. A former data analyst on Bridge and Deluge who read housing collapse patterns in municipal permit data. The age of the ripple-readers had begun, and Meridian's corporations were about to discover that the most dangerous thing in a city of secrets wasn't a journalist, or a whistleblower, or a hacker.

It was someone who could do math in the rain.

Mira walked home through the downpour, the notebook safe against her chest, and for the first time in years, she didn't mind the water. Let it come. Let it flood every corner of this city. Because water revealed what land concealed — the shapes beneath the surface, the ripples in the deep, the patterns that were always there for anyone willing to stand in the storm and look.

She had six weeks. She had a notebook full of ripples. And she had a choice that wasn't really a choice at all.

The truth was coming. The only question was whether she'd be the one who told it, or the one who read about it in someone else's byline.

She already knew the answer.
